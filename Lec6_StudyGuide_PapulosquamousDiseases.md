## 1. Introduction — Other Papulosquamous Diseases

### Summary

This lecture covers four diseases that share morphological overlap with Psoriasis (papulosquamous eruptions) but are distinct in aetiology, histopathology, and management:

1. **Pityriasis Rosea** — self-limited, HHV-related
2. **Pityriasis Rubra Pilaris (PRP)** — CARD14 mutation, Vitamin A derivative treatment
3. **PLEVA** (Pityriasis Lichenoides et Varioliformis Acuta) — acute, clonal T-cell
4. **PLC** (Pityriasis Lichenoides Chronica) — chronic end of same spectrum

> **💡 The "GPSS" Mnemonic — 4 Diseases That Look Alike:**
> These four conditions are in each other's differential diagnosis and must always be considered together:
> - **G** = Guttate Psoriasis
> - **P** = Pityriasis Rosea
> - **S** = Secondary Syphilis (Pityriasiform eruption)
> - **S** = PLEVA / PLC (formerly "PLC")
> When you see any one of these, think of the other three.

## 2. Pityriasis Rosea — Definition, Aetiology & Clinical Features

### Summary

#### Definition
- A **self-limited papulosquamous eruption** that is occasionally pruritic
- Primarily affects **adolescents and young adults**
- **Female predominance**
- Favours the **trunk and proximal extremities**

#### Aetiology
- Associated with **Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) and Human Herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7)**
- **Important distinction:** Pityriasis Rosea is **NOT a primary viral infection** — the virus is not the direct cause
- It is an **immunological reaction** — certain individuals, when exposed to HHV-6 or HHV-7, develop an immune dysregulation that manifests as Pityriasis Rosea
- This explains why **antiviral therapy (Acyclovir)** can be used in severe cases

#### The Herald Patch — The Beginning
- Pityriasis Rosea starts with a **single solitary lesion** called the **Herald Patch** (also called the "Mother Patch")
- Herald Patch characteristics:
  - **Sharply defined erythematous patch or thin plaque**
  - **Rounded or oval shape**, 2–6 cm in diameter
  - Covered with **Collarette Scales** (the pathognomonic scale type)
  - Most commonly found on the **thigh, upper trunk, or back**
- The Herald Patch appears **days to weeks** before the generalised eruption

#### Collarette Scales — The Pathognomonic Feature
- **Collarette = a ring/collar of scales** at the periphery of the plaque
- The scales face **inward** toward the centre of the lesion (like a collar or frill)
- This is the **most important feature** distinguishing Pityriasis Rosea from its differentials:
  - Guttate Psoriasis → silvery-white scales
  - PLC → **Mica scales** (peel off in one complete piece)
  - Pityriasis Rosea → **Collarette scales**

#### Generalised Eruption
- Appears **days to weeks** after the Herald Patch
- Multiple **smaller oval lesions** — smaller than the Herald Patch
- Located mainly on **trunk and proximal extremities**
- Face and distal extremities usually **spared**
- Sun-exposed areas usually **spared**
- Distribution: **bilateral and symmetrical**
- The long axis of each lesion follows the **planes of cleavage (Langer's lines)**
- On the posterior trunk, lesions run parallel to ribs → **"Fir Tree" or "Christmas Tree" pattern**
- Lesions appear in **crops** (waves) every 7–10 days
- **Total duration:** typically 6–8 weeks (up to 2 months)

## 3. Pityriasis Rosea — Variants, Histopathology & Treatment

### Summary

#### Variants of Pityriasis Rosea

**By site:**
- **Generalised** — classic distribution (trunk + proximal limbs)
- **Inverse** — lesions appear in axillae and groin (intertriginous areas)
- **Localised** — very rare; limited to one area (difficult to diagnose clinically)

**By morphology (rare variants):**
- **Papular** — tiny papules with minimal scales; commonly seen with **drug-induced Pityriasis Rosea**
- **Vesicular, Pustular, Urticarial, Purpuric, Lichenoid** — extremely rare; usually only diagnosed by biopsy/exclusion

**By Herald Patch presence:**
- **Abortive** — only the Herald Patch appears; generalised eruption never develops (the disease "aborts")
- **Without Herald Patch** — generalised eruption appears without a preceding Herald Patch

#### Drug-Induced Pityriasis Rosea
Drugs that can trigger a Pityriasiform eruption:
- **ACE inhibitors**
- **Beta-blockers**
- **Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**
- **BCG vaccine**
- **Gold**
- **Isotretinoin**

> **💡 Comparison with Psoriasis-triggering drugs (TIEPOD-LIBA from Lec3):**
> Note the overlap — Beta-blockers appear in both lists. When a patient on Beta-blockers develops a papulosquamous eruption, consider BOTH Psoriasis and Pityriasis Rosea as possibilities.

#### Histopathology — Non-Specific (but recognisable)
Pityriasis Rosea histopathology is **non-specific** — it will NOT give a definitive diagnosis, but shows characteristic features:

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Small mounds of Parakeratosis** | Small foci of retained nuclei in stratum corneum (much less than Psoriasis) |
| **Mild Acanthosis** | Epidermal thickening (less regular/less prominent than Psoriasis) |
| **Spongiosis** | Intercellular oedema between keratinocytes → the hallmark of **acute eczema** (shared with Pityriasis Rosea) |
| **Mild lymphocytic perivascular infiltrate** | Lymphocytes around blood vessels in the dermis |
| **Interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate** | Lymphocytes scattered between collagen fibres |
| **Erythrocyte extravasation** | Small number of RBCs outside vessels (not clinically apparent) |

> **💡 Key Histopathology Concept — Spongiosis:**
> - Spongiosis = **intercellular oedema** (fluid between keratinocytes), causing them to separate and appear pale/vacuolated
> - Most characteristic of: **Acute Eczema**
> - Also seen in Pityriasis Rosea → makes it look eczematous on biopsy
> - The combination of Parakeratosis + Spongiosis + Mild Acanthosis places Pityriasis Rosea between Psoriasis and Eczema in the histopathological spectrum

#### Treatment
- **Most patients need NO treatment** — the condition is self-limited
- **First step always:** Reassurance + Patient education (the condition resolves in 6–8 weeks)
- **Symptomatic treatment for pruritus:**
  - **Emollients** (moisturisers)
  - **Mild topical corticosteroids** (e.g., Hydrocortisone — avoid potent steroids like Betamethasone/Mometasone)
  - **Antihistamines** (for itch)
  - Loose cotton clothing, cool showers
- **Severe Pityriasis Rosea:**
  - **UVB phototherapy**
  - **Systemic corticosteroids**
  - **Acyclovir** (antiviral — targets HHV-6 and HHV-7 association)
  - **Erythromycin** (antibiotic — used especially in PLC as well)

## 4. Pityriasis Rubra Pilaris (PRP) — Definition & Clinical Features

### Summary

#### Definition
**Pityriasis Rubra Pilaris (PRP)** = a papulosquamous disorder characterised by:
- **Hyperkeratotic follicular papules on an erythematous base**
- Papules coalesce to form **orange-red plaques** with **islands of normal skin**
- Associated with characteristic Palmoplantar and Scalp changes

> **Translation of the name:**
> - **Pityriasis** = scaly
> - **Rubra** = red/erythematous
> - **Pilaris** = related to hair follicles

#### The 5 Key Clinical Hallmarks of PRP

| Hallmark | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Nutmeg Grater sign** | Skin feels rough like a nutmeg grater due to follicular hyperkeratotic papules — individual follicular spines palpable |
| **2. Islands of Normal Skin** | Within the erythematous/scaly eruption, there are distinct areas of completely **normal skin** — pathognomonic |
| **3. Sandal Keratoderma** | Waxy, **yellow-orange Palmoplantar Keratoderma** — looks like the patient is wearing a yellow sandal |
| **4. Scaly Scalp** | Diffuse scalp scaling (resembles seborrhoeic dermatitis) |
| **5. Thickened Nails** | Nail thickening (not pitting — unlike Psoriasis which gives PDRL) |

> **⚠️ Key Distinction — PRP Nails vs Psoriasis Nails:**
> - **Psoriasis nails:** PDRL (Pitting, Dystrophy, Red spot, Leukonychia) + HOOS (Hyperkeratosis, Onycholysis, Oil drop, Splinter haemorrhage)
> - **PRP nails:** **Thickening** — NOT pitting

#### Age Groups
- **Bimodal distribution:**
  - **Adults:** 4th to 6th decade (40–60 years)
  - **Children:** 1st to 2nd decade (< 20 years)
- Equal sex distribution (M = F)

#### Aetiology (Etiopathogenesis)
- Largely **unknown**
- **CARD14 gene mutation** — the most important genetic association; must be memorised
- Possible contributing factors:
  - **Vitamin A deficiency** (explains why Vitamin A derivatives are the main treatment)
  - Disorder of keratinisation
  - Autoimmune mechanism

> **💡 CARD14 Mutation — Exam Pearl:**
> CARD14 mutations are also seen in **Psoriasis** (specifically Familial Psoriasis). So both Psoriasis and PRP can share this genetic background, further explaining their clinical overlap.

## 5. PRP — Classification (6 Types), Histopathology & Treatment

### Summary

#### Classification — 6 Types (Griffiths Classification)

| Type | Category | Key Features | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type 1** | Adult Classic (Generalised) | Red-orange plaques, islands of sparing, spreads caudally, sandal keratoderma, nutmeg grater | **55%** — Most common |
| **Type 2** | Adult Atypical (Generalised) | + Lamellar scaling (like lamellar ichthyosis), eczema, alopecia | 5% |
| **Type 3** | Juvenile Classic (Generalised) | Same as Type 1 in children; peaks in first 2 years + adolescence | 10% |
| **Type 4** | Juvenile Focal | Follicular hyperkeratosis + erythema on **elbows and knees**; pre-pubertal onset | 25% |
| **Type 5** | Juvenile Atypical | Follicular hyperkeratosis + erythema + **scleroderma-like changes**; accounts for most familial cases | < 5% |
| **Type 6** | HIV-associated (Variable) | **Follicular spines** + **Acne conglobata** + **Hidradenitis suppurativa**; severe, generalised, treatment-resistant | 1% (very rare) |

> **💡 Type 6 = HIV Association — High-Yield Exam Pearl:**
> If you see PRP that is:
> - **Severe and widespread**
> - **Treatment-resistant**
> - Has **follicular spines** (sharp, spiky follicular papules)
> - Associated with **Acne conglobata** AND/OR **Hidradenitis suppurativa**
>
> → **Think HIV.** Type 6 PRP is one of the cutaneous manifestations of HIV.

#### Histopathology — The Checkerboard Pattern (Pathognomonic)

PRP has one of the **most characteristic histopathological patterns** in dermatology:

**Two components:**

**1. Hair Follicle Changes:**
- **Orthokeratotic plug** filling the follicle (follicular plugging)
- **"Shouldering" of Parakeratosis** — parakeratosis flanking BOTH sides of the follicular ostium (the keratin column is surrounded on left and right by parakeratotic cells)

**2. Interfollicular Epidermis — The Checkerboard:**
- **Alternating Orthokeratosis and Parakeratosis** — both vertically (in columns) and horizontally (in rows)
- This creates a **checkerboard/chessboard pattern** — areas with retained nuclei (parakeratosis) alternate with areas without (orthokeratosis) like black and white squares on a chess board
- Also: **Hypergranulosis** (thickened granular cell layer — opposite to Psoriasis which has absent granular layer)
- **Acanthosis** (epidermal thickening)
- **Perifollicular lymphocytic infiltrate**

> **🧠 PRP Histopathology Summary (memorise):**
> 1. Follicular plugging (orthokeratotic plug)
> 2. Shouldering of parakeratosis around follicle
> 3. **Checkerboard/Chessboard pattern** (alternating ortho + parakeratosis, vertical + horizontal)
> 4. Hypergranulosis
> 5. Acanthosis
> 6. Perifollicular lymphocytic infiltrate

#### Differential Diagnosis of PRP
- **Psoriasis** (most important — both give erythematous scaly plaques over large BSA)
- **Seborrhoeic Dermatitis** (especially for scalp involvement)
- **Eczema**

#### Treatment
- **Isotretinoin** — **Drug of choice**, at a **higher dose than for acne:**
  - Normal acne dose: 0.5–1 mg/kg/day
  - PRP dose: **1.5–2 mg/kg/day**
- **Acitretin** (systemic retinoid — Vitamin A derivative)
- **Methotrexate** (systemic immunosuppressant)
- **Topical treatments:** Retinoids, Calcineurin inhibitors, Vitamin D analogues (same as Psoriasis topicals)
- **Phototherapy:** Generally **NOT recommended** — can cause **aggravation** (worsening) of PRP
- **Natural course:** Self-limited — resolves in **3–5 years** without treatment in most cases

> **💡 Why Isotretinoin and NOT plain Vitamin A?**
> Although Vitamin A deficiency is a proposed mechanism, we do NOT give plain Vitamin A supplements. Instead, we give **Vitamin A derivatives (retinoids)** — Isotretinoin, Acitretin — because they have better tissue targeting and controlled activity compared to Vitamin A itself.

## 6. PLEVA and PLC — The Pityriasis Lichenoides Spectrum

### Summary

#### Overview — A Spectrum, Not Two Separate Diseases
PLEVA and PLC represent the **two ends of a single disease spectrum** — the **Pityriasis Lichenoides spectrum:**

| Feature | PLEVA (Acute end) | PLC (Chronic end) |
|---|---|---|
| **Full name** | Pityriasis Lichenoides **et Varioliformis Acuta** | Pityriasis Lichenoides **Chronica** |
| **Nature** | Acute, severe inflammation | Chronic, milder inflammation |
| **Primary lesion** | Erythematous papules → **vesicles, pustules, haemorrhagic crusts, necrosis** | Erythematous papules with **Mica scales** |
| **Scale type** | Haemorrhagic crust, necrotic tissue | **Mica scales** (peel off in one complete piece) |
| **Histopathology** | **Interface dermatitis** (severe lymphocytic damage to BMZ) | Same but **milder** — no frank interface damage |
| **Treatment response** | Responds to Erythromycin, PUVA | Responds to Erythromycin, PUVA |

#### Pathological Classification — Clonal T-Cell Lymphoproliferative Disorder
Both PLEVA and PLC are classified as:
- **Clonal T-cell lymphoproliferative disorders**
- They involve **proliferation of T-lymphocytes** — similar to but not the same as frank malignancy
- The T-cell infiltrate is often **monoclonal** (arising from one clone)
- This is why they are sometimes classified under:
  - **Vascular chapter** (due to lymphocytic vasculitis component)
  - **Tumour chapter** (due to lymphoproliferative nature)
  - **Papulosquamous chapter** (clinical appearance)

> **💡 Why are these classified under multiple chapters?**
> - In Papulosquamous: because of the clinical presentation (papules + scales)
> - In Vascular: because there can be a **lymphocytic vasculitis** component
> - In Tumours: because they are clonal T-cell **lymphoproliferative** disorders

#### PLEVA — Clinical Features
- **Crops** of erythematous papules that rapidly evolve to:
  - Vesicles
  - Pustules
  - **Haemorrhagic vesicles/crusts** (from necrosis of papule tops)
  - Ulcers and necrotic tissue
- Widespread distribution — trunk and extremities
- Looks alarming — resembles **vasculitis or Chickenpox**

#### PLC — Clinical Features
- **Crops** of erythematous papules with **Mica scales** (scales that peel off entirely as a sheet)
- More chronic course
- Less dramatic than PLEVA
- Similar distribution to Pityriasis Rosea, Guttate Psoriasis

#### Histopathology — PLEVA vs PLC

**PLEVA:**
- **Interface Dermatitis** (most important feature) = **Vacuolar degeneration of the Basement Membrane Zone (Dermo-Epidermal Junction)**
- Dense lymphocytic infiltrate attacking the BMZ → damage → vesicle/blister/necrosis formation
- Predominantly **T-cell infiltrate**, often **monoclonal**
- Erythrocyte extravasation

**PLC:**
- Same pattern as PLEVA but **mild** — minimal interface damage
- Lymphocytic infiltrate present but less intense
- No frank vacuolar degeneration visible

> **💡 Interface Dermatitis — Key Concept:**
> Interface dermatitis = lymphocytes attacking the **Dermo-Epidermal Junction (basement membrane zone)**. When severe (as in PLEVA), this destroys the BMZ → vesicles, blisters, necrosis appear. When mild (PLC), only subtle changes. Other diseases with interface dermatitis include: Lichen Planus, Lupus Erythematosus, Dermatomyositis.

#### Differential Diagnoses

**PLEVA Differentials:**
- **Vasculitis** (purpuric papules, haemorrhagic vesicles, necrosis — VERY close mimicker)
- **Drug eruption** (similar widespread erythematous papules)
- **Chickenpox** (vesicular papules)
- **Lymphomatoid Papulosis** (most important — also a T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder; can be malignant)

**PLC Differentials (the GPSS group again):**
- **Pityriasis Rosea**
- **Guttate Psoriasis**
- **Secondary Syphilis**

#### Treatment
- **Erythromycin** (antibiotic — most commonly used; mechanism not fully understood)
- **PUVA** (phototherapy — effective for both PLEVA and PLC)
- **UVB** — also used
- **Methotrexate** (for severe/recalcitrant cases)
- **Tetracyclines** (alternative antibiotic)

#### High-Yield MCQ Facts

- HIV type: **Type 6 = follicular spines + acne conglobata + hidradenitis suppurativa**
- Nails: **Thickening** (not pitting like Psoriasis)
- Histopathology hallmark: **Checkerboard pattern (alternating ortho + parakeratosis, vertical + horizontal)**
- Additional histopath: Follicular plugging, shouldering, hypergranulosis, acanthosis
- Treatment: **Isotretinoin 1.5–2 mg/kg** (higher than acne dose of 0.5–1 mg/kg)
- Phototherapy: **Contraindicated / can worsen PRP**

**PLEVA:**
- Full name: Pityriasis Lichenoides **et Varioliformis Acuta**
- Classification: **Clonal T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder**
- Histopath: **Interface dermatitis** (vacuolar degeneration of BMZ)
- Key differential: **Lymphomatoid Papulosis** (most specific important DD)
- Treatment: Erythromycin, PUVA

**PLC:**
- Full name: Pityriasis Lichenoides **Chronica**
- Scale: **Mica scales** (peel off as complete sheets)
- Histopath: Same as PLEVA but **milder** interface changes
- Differentials: **GPSS group** (Guttate Psoriasis, Pityriasis Rosea, Secondary Syphilis)
- Treatment: Erythromycin, PUVA

#### The GPSS Cross-Differential Group
These four always appear in each other's differential — whenever you see one, think of the others:

| G | P | S | S |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Guttate Psoriasis** | **Pityriasis Rosea** | **Secondary Syphilis** | **PLEVA/PLC** |
| Silvery-white scales | Collarette scales | Pityriasiform, palm/sole involvement | Mica scales (PLC) / Haemorrhagic (PLEVA) |
